The cost case, and the budget it decides

Two independently written drivers measured this, on a sun_debug node, one core, fresh process per variant, median of three, imports resolved before the timer. They agree within 3 % on every arrangement, which is the reason to trust the table rather than either driver.

The single decision-relevant line: the exact-everywhere quadrature is the one arrangement that misses the first-build budget on a single core, and the closed form is the only exact lane that makes it there. That is why the shipped default is exact everywhere in closed form and not a banded scheme — the banded arrangements are cheaper still, but they approximate, and there is now no budget reason to. An independent run through the shipped element dispatch on a 548-cell grid reproduced these at 900.7 / 176.4 / 63.0 / 116.6 µs/pair and put the closed-form 2000-cell build at 11.8 min on one core.

The point build reproduces its tracked 963 ms at 1.16 s, the difference being that the tracked figure repeats the solve in one warm process and a fresh one costs 1.20× that — the same in-process warmup that makes every number here a fresh-process one.

The finding that redirected the design: the two kernels cross at 64 pairs

The two flags were written as independent — one chooses how pairs are binned, the other what the exact treatment is — and in MEANING they are. In COST they are not, and the measurement is unambiguous. Cost against distance is flat for both, the closed form a constant 0.34–0.35× the quadrature from 0.2 to 29 section radii, so a per-pair rate may be quoted for a whole column with no distance weighting. Cost against batch width is not flat at all.

The quadrature builds one angular rule and reuses it across the batch, so it improves 1.3× over that whole range. The closed form holds up to three corner parts live and amortises them, so it improves 38×. An exact-everywhere column hands the kernel all 2,339 pairs at once and the closed form wins five-fold; the three-band scheme hands its near band about thirteen, an order below the crossing, and there the quadrature is 2.3× cheaper — which makes a banded build 1.9× dearer with the closed form on. Both docstrings now say so. It is deliberately not switched automatically on batch width: which kernel ran would then depend on how a caller happened to group its pairs, and a stored operator's values have to be reproducible from its geometry alone.

The band populations are what turn a per-band cost into a column rate, and a real grid is not the idealised section: its wall cuts the boundary ring into three- to twelve-corner cells and puts 12.1 % of pairs on the mid rule where an idealised hexagon puts 2.6 % (near 2.1 % / 0.56 %). That is the whole of why the banded rows in the build table sit above their column figures.

What the point filament was costing, measured against the closed form

On a real 333-cell hexagonal grid (261 regular, 72 wall-clipped) at R0 = 6.2 m, every plasma-plasma pair evaluated both ways. The reference is checked rather than assumed: against a 64×96 oracle, inside a quarter contour radius the closed form sits at 1.1e-12 median where the shipped (16, 48) rule sits at 5.3e-10 and reaches 5.8e-05, and refining the rule through (24,64), (32,80), (48,96), (64,96) to (96,128) takes BZ from 4.35e-02 to 1.35e-10 — converging onto the closed form, which is what says the quadrature is the error rather than the arbiter.

The recorded self-flux figure reproduces exactly: 7.206 % median (the record said 7.2 %), 15.3 % maximum against a recorded 18 % — the maximum is grid-dependent and the median is not. The point value is always low, on all 333 cells, with no sign flips: a filament concentrates the current where the section spreads it, so it under-reads its own flux systematically rather than noisily. Wall-clipped cells are worse than regular ones, 9.22 % median against 7.21 %.

The self-FIELD is the result the flux figure was hiding. On the diagonal the exact BZ is 9.8e-08 and the point model returns −2.0e-06: the wrong sign and twenty times the magnitude, a median 2152 % of local and 2.35× the whole column's peak. BR is zero on the diagonal by symmetry for a regular cell and both models agree there, but for a wall-clipped cell the point model is up to the column peak wrong. None of this is a divergence being mishandled — OffsetFilaments keeps the answer finite by pulling the source and target rings apart, measured here at a median 0.769 section radii. It is a finite, plausible, and wrong number, produced by an ad-hoc standoff, on every diagonal entry of the operator.

Refinement does not rescue it, and the two halves move in opposite directions — which is the subtlest thing measured here. From 180 to 1,274 cells the self-flux error falls only slowly, 7.72 % → 6.37 %, while the self-BZ error grows, 16× → 38× local, because a smaller cell puts its own centre relatively closer to its own boundary. The INTEGRATED error does fall, ψ 0.162 % → 0.024 % and BZ 3.41 % → 1.36 % of swing, because each cell carries less current. So refining the mesh dilutes the consequence without ever fixing the term: a 1,274-cell grid still misplaces 1.4 % of the BZ swing, and no achievable resolution takes the point model to a converged self-field.

The near band, which had no envelope until now

The banded scheme's near band used to be defined as bit-identical to the (16, 48) rule, so it carried no measured error of its own — everything the scheme was held to was the mid and far models'. With two kernels to choose between it needs one, and it now has one, measured on a real grid's own pair population, relative to the band's peak:

So the gain is real and it is extremely local: it applies to 0.031 % of a real grid's pairs (34 of 110,889), and the near band as a whole is 3.5 %. Beyond half a contour radius the two kernels agree to 9.5e-12 on the flux and 2.6e-11 on the field of peak, which is what lets either serve the band without moving a seam — both seam-jump bounds hold at 1e-6 for both kernels, and every mid- and far-band per-component envelope is unchanged. The 0.031 % is not a reason to dismiss it: 12 of those 34 pairs ARE the diagonal, and a plasma-plasma operator's diagonal is what its conditioning rests on.

A real grid is not the acceptance gate's tidy sections

Worth recording because it was the adoption risk, and it is now a test. The acceptance gate uses a hexagon, a rectangle, a trapezium and a thin plate. A real 179-cell grid's cells carry three to twelve corners, and wall clipping leaves edges as short as 1.6e-10 m beside edges of 0.12 m — coincident corners, in effect, nine orders apart. All 179 evaluate finite in all three components; a single non-finite entry would poison the whole operator, so that is pinned rather than assumed.

On that grid the worst off-diagonal disagreement between the two exact kernels is a neighbour's centre sitting 0.001 contour radii outside the source cell — which is what a hexagonal tiling does, a neighbour's centre can land against a shared edge — where the shipped rule is 2.9e-03 out on BZ. Refining it to 1024 panels closes that to 2.1e-12 of the closed form's value. Once again the closed form is what the quadrature converges to.

A consequence for cost that a synthetic hexagon hides: because the closed form is organised by corner, its rate tracks the corner count, so a grid gets cheaper per pair as it refines and the clipped fraction falls — mean corners 9.45 at 47 cells against 7.26 at 548. The 2000-cell projections above are conservative for that reason.

Worth stating plainly because it is the reason the flip is safe rather than brave: an interaction matrix is pure geometry, built once per machine and grid signature and cached, so steady-state solve throughput is untouched by this entirely. The whole cost of the change is a one-off build, and that build is inside its budget on a single core.

What is next

The plan's objective is met and its final rung has landed. Four followups remain, and none of them is this plan's subject. The one that matters to other work is the finite-arc polygon-section primitives (f-boa-03): the full turn is the special case in which every elliptic integral is complete, and polybow-arc-section and polybeam-prism-section both need the finite-arc counterpart, so they stay blocked on it. The other three are optimisations of an already-shipped route — the closed form's remaining cost floor (its residual node count and moment-stack length, f-boa-10), the graph behind its cold compile now that the compile is paid once (f-boa-13, and it shares the moment-stack lever with f-boa-10), and carrying the modulus complement into the element tier that Circle and Arc inherit (f-boa-12, which is a different tier's repair and gated on the full validation suite).

One thing this rung found and deliberately did not chase: the crossing at 64 pairs per call means the closed form's near-band cost is a batching artefact, not a kernel property. A near band evaluated across a whole tile rather than per source column would sit above the crossing and get the accuracy for free. That is a change to how the banded scheme is called, not to either kernel, and it belongs with whoever next touches the tiled assembly's binning.
