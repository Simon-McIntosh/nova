The three answers. How fast: 12 to 14 labels a second per GPU once a program is hot, 0.06 to 0.21 a second as the route stands, because the currents, the reduced coordinates and the constraint target are trace constants and every slice compiles its own program (56 compiles in the free arm, 47 in the conditioned). Sufficient for online training: no. The decoder consumes 8 frames a second per process, 125 ms a label, against 72 to 82 ms per warm label, so one hot program could feed one process with no margin for decode, transfer or host overhead, and the as-built rate cannot feed it at all. Written ahead: yes, once per shot to file in the session shape, so the solve rate bounds the corpus build, about 15 GPU-hours warm for the decoder corpus on one H200, not the training read.

The height pin did not raise throughput. The lead authorised the EFIT centre height as a stabilising constraint to raise throughput. Measured, the conditioned arm solves fewer slices (47 against 56), pays three and a half times the as-built wall (a heavier augmented program per slice) and runs at the same warm rate; what it buys is qualification, every converged conditioned slice topology-consistent on its branch, where the free arm's qualification was not reported. Every conditioned label also inherits one reconstruction scalar per slice on the axis position, so a held-out figure scored by the EFIT referee on such labels is flattered on exactly that number; the flag and target source carried beside each slice are the removal mechanism, and the pin is for the demo until a Thomson forward model supplies the height.

Batching does not help; tracing the currents does. A vmap of the fused trip over 8 and 16 states of one program runs 1.49 and 1.57 times slower per element than the serial loop (0.257 against 0.173 s, 0.067 against 0.043 s per slice), since the fused close is a write-then-read topology pass whose per-element work does not amortise; a cross-slice batch does not exist because distinct currents are distinct programs and the Newton ladder is a host loop. The traced-target change on main lets a moved target reuse the program but leaves the prescribed currents as trace constants, so the labeller's per-slice compile stands on main today. The rate lever is one program per shot with the currents traced, recorded as the next followup; the dataset write waits on it.
