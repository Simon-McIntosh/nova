The carrier is the same carrier on every device

Two changes make the diverted 300-cell certificate carrier device independent. The reference solve now runs through one ordered routine, so its coefficients are bit-identical wherever it runs, and the moment geometry identifies a lattice vertex by the cell slots that share it rather than by a quantised coordinate.

These reproduce the diagnosis recorded at merge 57c31ff1e exactly: 1075 against 1086 sample coordinates, 1536 against 1547 nodes, and a coefficient vector that differs by 3.9e-14 at its largest element between the two devices. The constraint system the reference solves has condition number 1.58e5, so a last-bit difference in the library solve becomes 4e-14 in the coefficients and moves every wall and cell position downstream.

A vertex is the slot set that shares it

MomentGeometry.from_cells merged sampling and atomic vertices by rint(vertex / tolerance). A shared corner is reconstructed once per cell that carries it, so the copies differ in their last bits; two copies merge only while they fall inside one bin, and a bin boundary between them is decided by the phase the reference solve happens to produce. The merge now groups slots through a coordinate spatial hash whose cells are 1e-9 of the lattice scale wide, and it reads no neighbour rings and no cell order at all. That reach is a millionth of the distance between neighbouring corners and a thousand times the 7.4e-13 m drift the reference solve was observed to produce, and every slot is compared against the hash cells on both sides of its own, so no bin edge can separate a pair. A candidate group is rejected when its own spread reaches the merge width, which keeps a wall-clipped sliver from collapsing into a degenerate polygon, and two slots of one cell are never joined. Merged polygon vertices carry one stored coordinate — the mean of the copies taken in sorted order — so the atomic mesh that atomises them keys on equal values instead of on a bin edge, and the stored value does not depend on which cell was listed first.

The vertex identity is compared as a partition, not as the numeric id array: the two devices list the same cells in a different order, so a slot's id is not comparable between them. Each slot is named by the micron cell of its own centre and the comparison asks which slots share a vertex, which a cell reordering does not disturb.

The reference solve is solved twice in fresh processes at OMP_NUM_THREADS, OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS and MKL_NUM_THREADS of 1 and 4, and the coefficients, magnetic axis and separatrix are required to be bit-identical. The solve publishes coefficient_digest, the sha256 of its coefficient vector as stored binary64, so a carrier records the reference it was built from.

The thread-count arm alone does not carry the claim, since one machine usually gives the same answer at one and four threads either way. The digest itself is therefore compared across devices: the CPU and the H200 both publish 01818d5d423fe502. The control is the library solve this replaced. Restoring np.linalg.solve in a scratch copy of the head tree gives 3b71e589179c036f on the CPU against 01818d5d423fe502 from the H200 receipt, so the digests disagree exactly when the driver chooses the elimination order (logs/determinism-control-library-solve.log).

One identity, chosen by value

The first repair left two identities in the tree. PlasmaGrid merged sample vertices by a quantised coordinate while MomentGeometry merged them through the mesh's neighbour rings, so one lattice produced 242 sample nodes through the grid and 318 through the geometry, and tests/test_biotpolysection.py — which requires the two to agree — passed at base and failed at head. Both now call one function, stencil_mesh.lattice_vertex_index.

That function reads no adjacency at all. A lattice vertex is the set of cell slots whose coordinates agree within 1e-9 of the lattice scale, found through a spatial hash at that width. A candidate group is rejected when its own spread reaches the merge width, which keeps a wall-clipped sliver from collapsing, and two slots of one cell are never joined. Because the answer depends on the coordinates and not on which of the mesh's rings happened to carry them, a grid that lists one lattice in a different order — which the two devices do — keeps the same identity.

The stored coordinate of a merged corner is the mean of its copies taken in sorted order, not the first copy in list order, and the sample coordinate array is published sorted. Both remove the last dependence on cell order, so two builds of one lattice publish the same sample_node_coordinates, the same atomic nodes and the same second moments.

The atomiser is a second quantisation

The topology read builds its own carrier: TopologyGeometry.from_cells atomises the cell polygons through AtomicCellMesh.from_cells, whose node key is a quantised coordinate. That key is a second binned identity, and it split a shared corner into two nodes on the diverted closed-form carrier. The cells carry six corners each; the copies of one corner differ in their last bits because each cell recomputes it from its own centre, and the vessel centroid those centres are built from moved in the last bit when the reference solve began running through the ordered routine. The bins followed.

Measured on the diverted closed-form carrier at 132 cells: at the base the carrier has no cell carrying a repeated vertex, and at the merged head 70 of the 132 do, with a maximum of 11 vertices in one cell against 10 before. The split lands in the column of cells at x = 1.5722 (cells 48 to 53). Cell 50 reads a membership fraction of 0.011 where the reference quadrature gives 1.0, and the X-point is not admitted: the saddle sits at (1.502528, −1.01728), 0.0256 m from the centre of cell 49, and boundary_class reads 0 where the read needs 1. The contour tree grows the padding to 264 node rows, and the extra row reads 8.3e-32 in the eager path against 7.1e-3 in the compiled one, which is the trace mismatch.

TopologyGeometry.from_cells now rewrites its cells through canonical_polygons — the same merge the moment geometry uses — before atomising. The copies of a corner then carry one value, so no bin edge can separate them: duplicate-vertex cells fall from 70 to 0 and the maximum cell vertex count from 11 to 10, and the three tests pass at their exact tolerances (logs/fix-three-tests.log, 5 passed). The cross-device receipt arm and the perturbed-wall arm still pass (51 passed across the carrier, stencil-mesh and poly-section files, logs/fix-device-and-identity.log).

AtomicCellMesh itself still keys on a quantised coordinate, which is the mechanism that split the corner. The canonical input makes that key unable to separate two copies of one lattice vertex, and leaves the atomiser's own cell-internal split behaviour to the node that owns separatrix_clip.py.

What the tests now require

The test that compared two outputs of one build now counts the lattice vertices from the cell polygons instead: every cell lists every corner it carries, so the number of distinct positions in their union is the lattice's vertex count, measured outside the geometry. It reads 1075, and the geometry publishes 1075. A new test permutes the cells and requires the sample coordinates, the atomic node coordinates and the second moments to be identical to the unpermuted build.

Carrier fixture rebuild

The carrier identity hashes the sources in CARRIER_SOURCE_PATHS, which lists nova/equilibrium/stencil_mesh.py but not nova/equilibrium/analytic_single_null.py: the reference reaches the identity through the wall content it emits, not through its own source bytes. The digest moved from 76aacb2ebf7dddd3 at the base of this repair to f775d83e9fc3b0d4 at its head, so every cached carrier rebuilds once. Because the identity hashes source bytes, a comment in stencil_mesh.py moves it too: 20f042fedd3d237c was taken before the docstring corrections landed and is not the head's value.

The rebuild of one row is measured on the compute node in fresh processes (logs/carrier-fixture-rebuild.log, job 1294764). The coarse fixture carries 551 cells. Cold, the carrier builds in 104.5 s and the row takes 245.3 s wall; warm, the same cache key hits (build_s has no entry) and the row takes 125.0 s. The warm hit is the control that the key is stable inside one tree, so the move above is a real invalidation rather than a recompute on every run.

One residual device difference is outside this node's write scope: the atomic vertex count reads 923 on the CPU and 922 on the H200 at the head, because AtomicCellMesh.from_cells in nova/equilibrium/separatrix_clip.py still atomises by a quantised coordinate key. It does not enter node_number, which counts the grid, wall and sample vertices, and those agree exactly.

The canonical corner costs a value-matrix rebuild, not a booking

Rewriting a shared corner to one value moves the geometry the read poses its Gauss rule on, and the dense value matrix is cached on that rule's bytes. Measured on the diverted carrier at 250 cells, timing each stage of the prototype build on its own cores: from_cells costs 0.37 s at the reviewed head and 0.38 s here, the compiled read 6.46 s against 6.54 s, current.book 2.51 s against 2.44 s, and the triangle rule 0.02 s as against 0.02 s. Every arithmetic stage agrees. The build dies in build_values, which costs 0.22 s against the warm cache and was still running at 480 s.

The cells are not the difference. The polygon bytes handed to the value matrix are identical at both revisions (60d11d75ed1c1bd9); what moves is the rule, by at most 8.9e-16 in the point coordinates (3760 of 10152 entries) and 4.4e-16 in the centres (184 of 564). The matrix key is a sha256 over those raw bytes, so the digest moves from e1afb81b137ef38e to d1e1e0926e8d6d4e and the whole (5076, 282, 3) matrix is reassembled through build_flux_blocks. A stack dump taken during the hang sits in values.py in assemble, reached from operand_matrix.

So the census slowdown is a cold operand-cache fill that any change to this geometry incurs, not work the canonical corners added. It cannot be removed inside stencil_mesh.py: the key is byte-exact, so every representative choice -- the mean of the copies, the first in sorted order, or any other -- moves it. The line to change is the operand key in scripts/prototypes/lean_map/values.py and the quadrature's operand_key, which is outside this node's write paths and is named here rather than edited.

A geometry edit that moves the Gauss rule owes a warm-up

The value matrix is content-addressed on purpose: the key is a digest of the Gauss rule, the source polygons and the two builder sources, and a miss publishes the matrix it assembles, so the next build at that revision hits. That is the behaviour the persistence test pins, and it is why the base arm looks fast -- its entry was published long ago. It also means a change that moves the rule by a single ulp is indistinguishable from a change that rebuilds every polygon, and the assembly is far more expensive than the tests that consume it: a cold rebuild of the (5076, 282, 3) matrix ran past 19 minutes on eight dedicated cores, where the three census tests are given 900, 300 and 900 seconds in total.

So any later edit that moves the Gauss rule owes the same warm-up the certificate fixtures owe: build once at the new digest, unbounded, letting the entry publish under the shared operand root, before timing or gating anything that consumes it. A test that runs on a cold cache is measuring polygon assembly, not the topology read, and a timeout taken there says nothing about either. This applies to any change to the canonical corner values, the cell polygons or the atomiser, not only to this one.
