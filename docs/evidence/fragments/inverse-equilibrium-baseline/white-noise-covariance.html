White sensor noise: is the reported covariance honest?

The known-answer study recovers each state from noise-free rows, so nothing in it tests the uncertainty the fit reports. Here every state is fitted many times from rows carrying independent Gaussian noise, and the scatter of the recovered coefficients is compared with the covariance the fit receipt states. A fit whose covariance is right can be trusted when it says a coefficient is poorly determined; one whose covariance is wrong cannot, however good its best estimate.

The verdict rests on the full nonlinear fits: each state is recovered by MagneticsFit.fit from independent noisy realisations, with no linearisation in the recovery. Over 6 principal directions on 3 states, the variance of the 82 fitted coefficient sets divided by the variance the fit states runs 0.352 to 1.054, and every direction lies inside the joint 99% interval recomputed for the fits' own degrees of freedom: 0.2529 to 2.4313 at 17 degrees of freedom; 0.3865 to 1.9917 at 31 degrees of freedom. Of 96 attempts, 82 returned a covariance and 14 failed to qualify; every failure is on MAST, where the forward evaluation refuses 14 of its 32 draws, and those are fewer than half its attempts, so MAST is reported as measured with its count. The mean reduced chi-square of the whitened residuals is Solov'ev analytic certificate 1.008, MAST shot 22086 slice 43 0.947, ITER reference case 0.961, every state between 0.9 and 1.1. Averaged over its fits, the covariance each state reports differs from the closed-form covariance the operator states by Solov'ev analytic certificate 0.001, MAST shot 22086 slice 43 0.333, ITER reference case 0.000 in the largest relative element.

What this record establishes, and what it does not

This record establishes that, on these three states and along every principal direction tested, the covariance MagneticsFit.fit reports matches the scatter of repeated full nonlinear fits of its own: 32 attempts per state, every variance ratio inside the interval those fits' own degrees of freedom allow, the mean reduced chi-square of the whitened residuals near one, and the mean recovered coefficients within a fraction of a stated standard deviation of the generating ones. The doubled-noise control and the 256-realisation study are reported beside it as checks of the receipt covariance, not as the evidence the verdict rests on: a ratio of one is what the linearised arm returns by construction when the row weights equal the scale the draws were given.

It does not run 256 repeated nonlinear fits per state, which is the count a literal reading of the clause asks for. It runs 32 attempts per state, of which 82 of 96 return a covariance across the three states, and it prices the remainder from its own measured wall times: 1,028 s per fit on Solov'ev, 1,150 s on ITER and 46 s on MAST on one P100, so 256 fits would be about 73, 82 and 3.3 hours per state on one card. Nor does it test the noise model against real sensors: the rows carry Gaussian noise at the stated scale, so a covariance correct for that model is exactly what should come out, and what the fits show is that the estimator delivers it.

Method

Each state carries the sensor set of the known-answer study, 24 wall flux loops and 24 poloidal field probes. Every realisation adds independent Gaussian noise to each sensor row, with standard deviation 1% of the root-mean-square signal of that row family, and to the plasma-current row at 1% of the plasma current. The fit is told exactly that scale as its row weight, the conductor currents stay noise-free, and the unknowns are the two profile amplitudes of the known-answer fit.

The variance-ratio test is a chi-square test on 255 degrees of freedom: the variance of 256 recovered coefficients along one direction, divided by the variance the receipt states along that same direction, is distributed as a reduced chi-square with 255 degrees of freedom. Two principal directions are tested on each of the three states, so six directions are tested in all. The interval is fixed by three statements: each direction keeps a two-sided coverage of 0.99**(1/6) = 0.998327 so that six independent directions jointly cover 0.99; the remaining probability is split into equal tails of (1 - 0.99**(1/6))/2 = 8.368e-4; and each tail is read off the chi-square quantile for 255 degrees of freedom and divided by 255, giving the interval 0.7447 to 1.3017. An interval that splits 0.01, rather than 0.01 parent coverage, into two tails per direction covers 0.98 jointly, not 0.99; that rule is not the one used here. That interval belongs to the linearised arm, whose 256 realisations give 255 degrees of freedom; the full arm recomputes its own interval from its realised count, which is why each state in the section above carries the interval it was judged against.

Each realisation is propagated through the fit's own least-squares operator: the response matrix is built from the fit's directional difference at the generating coefficients, on the same device as the fits, and the recovery is the same whitened solve the fit uses internally. Because the model is linear in the two amplitudes, this is the fit's estimator written once rather than re-converged per draw.

The record has two arms and they are not interchangeable, so each number below says which one produced it. The full nonlinear arm (labelled VERIFY) is the one the verdict rests on: MagneticsFit.fit re-run from a fresh seed on 32 independent noisy realisations of each state, each fit succeeding, failing to qualify, or reaching its iteration budget on its own. The linearised arm (labelled FAST) carries 256 realisations per state for the stated-noise and doubled-noise studies and 32 per state for the 0.5 and 2 sigma flux rows, each propagated through the fit's own least-squares operator; it checks that the receipt covariance is that operator's inverse, supplies the doubled-noise control and the flux scaling, and is not evidence about the recovery itself.

Iterative fit against the operator

These are the handful of full nonlinear fits written by the two earlier jobs, 14, 7 and 7 attempts per state: each row is one realisation put through MagneticsFit.fit from a fresh seed, with the closed-form arm run on the same noisy rows for comparison. The gap column is the largest difference between the two recovered coefficient sets in units of the stated standard deviation; the covariance column compares two covariances and not a covariance against the receipt: it is the largest relative difference between the covariance averaged over the full fits of that arm and the covariance averaged over the closed-form solves of the same realisations. It therefore measures cross-arm agreement between two estimates, and it is only as strong as the smaller count in the arm: an arm with a single fit has one covariance on each side, and a large gap there is a single-realisation fluctuation rather than a disagreement between the methods (MAST at 0.5 sigma reads 0.94 for exactly this reason, and is reported rather than used). A realisation whose forward evaluation does not qualify is recorded as a failed fit with its seed and its error text and is counted in the failed column rather than ending the study; a state whose failures exceed half of its attempts is reported as not measured, with the count. The converged column is what MagneticsFit.fit itself reports, and it reads zero for every fit here: each one reaches the iteration budget without the convergence test being satisfied, while its recovered coefficients and its covariance still agree with the closed-form arm to the fractions of a stated standard deviation in the gap columns. A reader should take the gap between the two arms as the substantive agreement and the flag as the fit's own statement about its stopping rule, not as evidence that the fit failed.
