One Jacobian-vector product costs more than one dense assembly, so the Krylov route is refused

The step's Jacobian is applied as one dense block. The alternative a carried equilibration would enable — reusing the previous update's row and column scales and cell-block inverses around support_read.step_jacobian_vector and letting GMRES solve the later updates — was measured before any change was made, because a later update needs about forty products and the product is only worth having if it is cheaper than the assembly it replaces. It is not. At sixteen lanes on the diverted 550-cell bank one batched product costs 106.68–107.16 ms of device time against 37.61–37.76 ms for one batched assembly, so the break-even is 0.35 iterations: a later update would have to converge in fewer than one product to tie. Forty products cost 4267.3–4286.6 ms, between 113 and 114 times one assembly.

The product is expensive for a structural reason, not a conditioning one. The assembly evaluates _local_booking_sensitivities once per cell and contracts all 3·cells bank channels through one einsum, so the booking sensitivity is computed once for the whole matrix; each product instead evaluates the full book_tangent for one direction. A single direction therefore costs more than the whole batched channel sweep. This is independent of the equilibration question the earlier matrix-free record settled: those scales cut the product path from 400 iterations to 40, and 40 is still about 114 assemblies' worth of products.

One H200 allocation, one GPU, four CPUs and 96 GB host memory. Each row is one fresh process that loaded the same saved lane arrays, excluded its own compile from the timed repetitions and reported the median of five synchronized calls. The read both arms sit behind is hoisted out of both timings. Batched rows fuse the lanes with vmap; the 128-lane batched graph is refused by the allocator, so that width is measured lane-sequentially under lax.map in the same compiled program.

At 128 lanes the fused assembly graph requests a 130.56 GiB temporary buffer and the allocator refuses it (RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED after rematerialization could only reach 121.48 GiB), which is the same memory wall the batch-width record met at width 256 for the whole solve. The lane-sequential arm in the same compiled program is the measurement that width can carry. It gives 1371.76 ms for the assembly against 1630.36 ms for the product at 128 lanes, a break-even of 0.841 iterations, and at sixteen lanes the same arm gives 175.32 ms against 202.65 ms, a break-even of 0.865. The two execution modes agree on the per-lane cost that matters here (10.72–10.96 ms assembly, 12.67–12.74 ms product), so the width does not change the decision: the product is above one assembly's cost at both widths and in both execution modes.

Every lane in both arms is the diverted 550-cell certificate state perturbed by the nested scale bank out to 128 lanes; the lane arrays are built once and saved, and both arms load the same file. The measurement is scripts/prototypes/bank_reused_scale_krylov/measure.py driven by device.sh. The internal shape check compares a (lanes, 3·cells, 3·cells) assembly block with three (lanes, cells) product components, 3·550 = 1650. The two batched processes ran at a revision whose check compared one product component against the full vector and so printed shape_consistent: false; that flag was the instrument's index error, not a shape disagreement, and the corrected check prints true in both lane-sequential rows. The timings are unaffected, and the correction is commit 1c550adc7182b3baf639748acac28f369f501bbd.

Verdict: the measurement favours the dense block, which is what the code already does. No product path was implemented, support_read.solve_batch and newton_step are unchanged, and no test was added, because the condition the brief sets for proceeding — forty products cheaper than one assembly at both widths — is false at the first width measured and cannot become true at a wider one.
