§1 — Objective

Give nova a polygon cross-section straight segment. Beam (nova/biot/beam.py) computes the interaction of a 3D straight line element with a finite rectangular cross-section — width × height, in Cartesian coordinates, with closed-form Ax/Ay/Az and Bx/By/Bz. There is no polygon-section counterpart, so a straight conductor of any other cross-section must be approximated by a bundle of rectangles or by a filament. This plan supplies the missing element: a prism of arbitrary polygonal cross-section.

It is the sibling of polybow-arc-section, and the pair completes the polygon column of nova's element grid. The two are deliberately separate plans because they are not the same problem, and conflating them would mis-scope both.

§2 — Why this is the easy one

A straight conductor has no curvature, so there are no elliptic integrals. That single fact separates this plan from its sibling. The elliptic machinery in the axisymmetric and arc cases exists entirely to handle the toroidal integration around a curved path; a prism has none. The reduction is:

  1. Stokes reduces the cross-section area integral to a contour sum over the polygon's edges, exactly as in Urankar Part V's first step — this part is shared, and is already implemented for the ring in nova/biot/polygon.py.
  2. The remaining axial integral along the segment is elementary: the same arsinh and arctan family that Beam already carries for a rectangle, evaluated per edge instead of per face.

Hard constraint on the implementation: if the derivation reaches for an elliptic integral, a Jacobi function, or a moment recursion, it has gone wrong. Those belong to the curved cases. The expected cost class is Beam's, not the ring kernel's, and the expected accuracy is round-off throughout — a prism's field has no near-singular parameter confluence of the kind that dominated the axisymmetric work, because there is no modulus to approach unity.

§3 — Deliverables

  • A prismatic polygon-section evaluation of the vector potential and all three field components, in the Cartesian frame Beam uses.
  • An acceptance gate against a converged numerical reference, with the same contour-distance banding the axisymmetric gate uses (distance measured to the cross-section boundary, not its centroid — a polygon's field is not isotropic and a centroid distance mixes converged and unconverged targets).
  • Element class following the Beam pattern (Matrix dataclass, ClassVar name, axisymmetric = False) so the frame and groupset machinery can consume it.
  • Cost per pair measured on a compute node, fresh process, median of 3, beside Beam's own figure.

§4 — The three free oracles

  1. A rectangular polygon reproduces Beam to its own tolerance. This is the primary check and it is exact rather than asymptotic: the same geometry through two independent formulations.
  2. A converged direct quadrature over the cross-section, built the way the axisymmetric oracle was — raise the rule until it stops moving, and assert that it has, rather than assuming convergence.
  3. The large-radius limit of PolyBow, once the sibling plan lands: as the arc's major radius grows at fixed section and fixed arc length, the two must converge. Record the radius at which they agree to a stated tolerance rather than asserting a limit that has not been measured — this doubles as an independent check on the arc evaluation and is the reason to keep both plans aware of each other.

Degenerate cases to pin, inherited from the axisymmetric work where the analogues bit: an edge parallel to the segment axis; a target lying exactly on an edge or, worse, exactly on a vertex of the cross-section (in the axisymmetric case that configuration returned a non-finite value and had to be fixed, because a field grid aligned with section corners hits it by construction); and a target inside the conductor, which is the whole reason a finite-section element exists.

§ Decisions

Derive the prism reduction directly, or transcribe it from the Urankar series' own straight-conductor part if that part covers a general polygon rather than only a rectangle?

Derived directly from Stokes, the way the ring and finite-arc closed forms were: the cross-section area integral reduces to a contour sum over polygon edges and the remaining axial integral is elementary (arsinh + arctan). No published straight-conductor series covering a general polygon was needed; Beam's rectangle expressions pin the transcendental family and serve as the exact oracle.

Does the element class extend Beam , or supersede it once a rectangle is just a four-sided polygon evaluated by the same code?

PolyBeam is a peer of Beam, both kept — the same relationship PolyBow has to Bow, and for the same reasons argued in nova/biot/polybow.py's docstring: the polygon element reads the frame's poly column rather than the width/height descriptor, so subclassing the rectangle class would inherit a body that must be disabled. Supersession is deferred until rectangle parity is pinned in production use.

§ Followups

Derive and land the prismatic polygon-section evaluation against Beam as its oracle

The whole plan is one coherent piece of work and small enough to land in a single rung: Stokes to a contour sum over the cross-section edges, an elementary axial integral per edge, and Beam as an exact oracle for the rectangular case. No elliptic machinery is involved; if the derivation reaches for it, it has gone wrong. Degenerate cases inherited from the axisymmetric work carry real risk -- particularly a target landing exactly on a cross-section vertex, which returned a non-finite value there and had to be fixed.
